Lessons from Studying Cloud Identity

March 2026

Certification exams have a reputation for testing trivia rather than understanding. Some of that reputation is earned. But studying for cloud identity certifications — and the CISSP before them — taught me things I hadn't fully articulated before, even after working in this space.

The value wasn't in memorizing protocol flows. It was in being forced to think systematically about topics I usually approach piecemeal.

Theory reveals what you've been assuming

When you work in an environment long enough, you develop intuitions about how things work. Some of those intuitions are correct. Some are wrong in ways you've never had reason to discover because the wrong version still produced the outcome you wanted.

Studying OAuth 2.0 flows in depth — all the grant types, not just the ones I use daily — revealed a few places where my mental model had gaps. The device authorization grant is a good example: I knew it existed, but hadn't thought carefully about the security properties that make it appropriate for constrained devices versus the risks it introduces if you apply it too broadly. The exam forces you to think through scenarios you haven't encountered yet.

Architecture decisions look different from the top

The CISSP covers identity from a governance and architecture layer, not a configuration layer. That altitude shift is genuinely useful. Instead of asking "how do I configure this," you're asking "why does this control exist, and what does it fail to address?"

The IAM domain in particular pushed me to think about provisioning and deprovisioning as a lifecycle problem, not a one-time task. Access that isn't reviewed and revoked accumulates. Most privilege escalation paths I've seen aren't about breaking authentication — they're about exploiting access that should have been removed months earlier.

Entra ID specifics that certification prep skips

Vendor-neutral certifications deliberately avoid platform specifics. That's appropriate for what they're doing, but it means there's a translation layer between the conceptual model and what you actually configure in Entra ID.

A few things that helped bridge that gap: reading the Microsoft Identity Platform documentation alongside the study material (not instead of it), and then immediately building the thing I'd just read about in a test tenant. Reading about Conditional Access named locations and then spending an hour configuring one cements the concept in a way that reading alone doesn't.

What I'd tell someone starting out

Don't treat certification prep as test prep. Treat it as a structured reading list with a forcing function at the end. The value is in the coverage and the depth, not in knowing which answer is correct for the scenario on page 47.

And build things while you study. The concepts that stick are the ones you've seen fail and had to fix.
